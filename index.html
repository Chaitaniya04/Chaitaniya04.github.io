Project 1

Why do 1 in 12 orders arrive late, and where should an operations team act first?

I analysed about 99,000 real orders from Olist, a Brazilian marketplace that connects small sellers with customers. The question: where does late delivery come from, and what does it cost in customer satisfaction?

Recommendations

  • Work with carriers on the slow lanes first. Transit, not seller handling, explains most late orders. Start with Maranhao, Ceara, Bahia and Rio de Janeiro, where late rates are 2 to 3 times Sao Paulo.
  • Set the promise by lane. One estimate for all routes hides the weak ones. Add a buffer on lanes with a history of lateness so the promise is kept.
  • Coach the slowest sellers. 101 of 2,948 sellers account for half of all late orders, and 419 account for 80%. A short watch list with a 3 day handover target is a small, focused action.
  • Plan capacity before peaks. Black Friday 2017 and the early 2018 months show lateness rises when volume or carrier load jumps.

Method and limits

Data: Olist Brazilian e-commerce public dataset, orders from late 2016 to August 2018, joined at order level in Python (pandas). I counted an order as late when it reached the customer after the estimated delivery date, and only looked at orders with status delivered. Seller concentration partly reflects order volume, so the next step would be to rank sellers by late rate with a minimum order count. I did not have carrier names, so lane level conclusions are by customer state and seller location. This is a public dataset, not data from an employer.

Project 2

How do a retailer and an electronics maker run their supply chains differently, and what should others take from it?

My MSc dissertation at Kingston University London (supervisor Dr Andy Lung, September 2024). I built a five criteria model to assess supply chain strategy and applied it to Tesco and Samsung Electronics, using secondary data and a comparative case study method.

CriterionTesco (retail)Samsung (manufacturing)
Cost efficiencyLean logistics, vendor managed inventoryLean manufacturing, just in time production
ResponsivenessAgile supply chain that follows consumer demandAgile manufacturing for fast product launches
FlexibilityFlexible sourcing for seasonal demandVertical integration and a diverse supplier network
SustainabilityCarbon cuts, waste reduction, sustainable sourcingEfficient manufacturing, ethical sourcing, energy efficiency
Risk managementSupplier collaboration, diversification, inventory controlAdvanced technology and monitoring

What I took from it

  • The same five criteria work in both sectors, but the right mix differs. Retail leans on lean and agile methods, while electronics leans on just in time production and innovation.
  • The model carries over to other industries such as automotive, healthcare and agriculture, because every sector must balance cost, speed, flexibility, sustainability and risk.
  • Risk planning for the UK food chain covers Brexit delays, labour shortages and climate disruption, with mitigations like supplier diversification, buffer stock and automation.

Limits: the study is qualitative and built on published sources, with no company internal data or interviews. That is why my portfolio also includes a project on real order data (Project 1).
