Case study, real data

Where do late deliveries come from, and what do they cost?

I analysed about 99,000 orders from Olist, a Brazilian marketplace that connects small sellers with customers. I joined the order, item, seller and review data in Python (pandas), defined late as arriving after the estimated delivery date, and looked only at delivered orders.

What I would do first

Fix the slow lanes

Transit explains most late orders. Start with Maranhao, Ceara, Bahia and Rio, where late rates run 2 to 3 times Sao Paulo.

Promise by lane

One estimate for every route hides the weak ones. Add a buffer on lanes with a history of lateness so the promise holds.

Coach 101 sellers

101 of 2,948 sellers cause half of late orders, and 419 cause 80%. A watch list with a 3 day handover target is a small, focused action.

Plan for peaks

Black Friday 2017 and early 2018 show lateness rises when volume jumps. Book carrier capacity before the peak.

Limits: a public dataset, not employer data. Seller concentration partly reflects order volume, so the next step is to rank sellers by late rate with a minimum order count. The data has no carrier names, so lane conclusions are by customer state and seller location.

Case study, MSc dissertation

How do a retailer and an electronics maker run supply chains differently?

My dissertation at Kingston University London (supervisor Dr Andy Lung, September 2024). I built a five criteria model to assess supply chain strategy and applied it to Tesco and Samsung Electronics, using secondary data and a comparative case study method.

CriterionTesco (retail)Samsung (manufacturing)
Cost efficiencyLean logistics, vendor managed inventoryLean manufacturing, just in time production
ResponsivenessAgile supply chain that follows demandAgile manufacturing for fast launches
FlexibilityFlexible sourcing for seasonal demandVertical integration, diverse suppliers
SustainabilityCarbon cuts, waste reduction, sustainable sourcingEfficient manufacturing, ethical sourcing
Risk managementSupplier collaboration, diversification, inventory controlAdvanced technology and monitoring

The same five criteria work in both sectors, but the right mix differs. Retail leans on lean and agile methods. Electronics leans on just in time production and innovation. The model carries to other industries because every sector balances cost, speed, flexibility, sustainability and risk.

Limits: qualitative, built on published sources, with no company internal data or interviews. That is why the order data analysis above exists.

Ranks suppliers on on time delivery, quality, lead time reliability and price stability. I am filling this same model with real data from the suppliers at Oneved.

On time score is the on time rate times 100. Quality loses 10 points per 1% defect rate. Lead time loses 10 points per day late. Price loses 5 points per 1% over quote. Strong is 85 and above, Watch is 70 to 84, below 70 is Act now.

Safety stock and reorder point Example data

When do I reorder, and how much buffer do I hold? It accounts for uneven demand and uneven supplier lead times, which simple calculators ignore.

0safety stock, units
0reorder point, units
0days of cover in the buffer

Safety stock is z times the square root of (lead time x demand variance + demand squared x lead time variance). Reorder point is average demand x lead time + safety stock. Cutting lead time spread often shrinks the buffer more than cutting demand spread, which is why supplier reliability matters.
